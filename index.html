Account-Assignment Workbench

Live artifact on each user's own connectors · capacity, fit and complexity checks · approved batches instead of one-by-one edits

Account moves between CSMs used to be assembled by hand in spreadsheets, then executed one account at a time in the CS platform so transition tasks fired. Nothing checked whether the receiving CSM was over capacity, whether the account matched the CSM's segment or region, whether a parent/child family was being split, or whether the account had already been passed around. A coverage-model transition earlier in the year surfaced roughly twice the accounts that had been planned for. A shared, live view of the book is what closes that gap.

The whole workflow runs in one place. Load a segment and pull shared roster status: leave, departing, hold, new hire, partial capacity. Pick a job: a new book for a new CSM or open req, reassign a departing or on-leave CSM, rebalance a team, or place accounts graduating from onboarding. Or describe the move in plain English, or paste a list. The tool ranks candidate CSMs by sales-pod alignment, capacity, complexity, region, language, and business-review load by quarter. Capacity is two checks, not one: parent-account cap and ARR cap are judged independently, because a CSM can have ARR headroom and still be at their account limit. The manager stages, overrides, and approves. Approved moves publish to a shared moves feed and go to Ops as one packet. Bulk moves export for the systems team, skip per-account tasks, and notify each receiving CSM directly.

Integrity queues find problems before they become misfired tasks: segment mismatches, region misalignment, accounts held by inactive or off-roster CSMs, CSM ownership disagreeing between the CS platform and the CRM, manager drift between the CS platform and the HR system, families split across CSMs, high-value accounts with no executive sponsor, stale engagement, temporary assignments past their return date, and accounts reassigned two or more times in 12 months.

Complexity comes from a segment director's account-load model, which I implemented: six weighted dimensions on a ~100-point scale (value tier 25, hierarchy depth 20, lifecycle stage 20, expansion potential 15, product breadth 10, health and risk 10), tiered High, Medium, and Low. The weights are a first pass and have not been validated against CSM time data.

Mid-Market asked for it, and got it in under a week, built from the segment's requirements doc: ARR bands, pooled coverage below a threshold treated as correct by design rather than flagged as a gap, per-person caps that follow the person rather than the segment, and a churn guard that refuses to stage an account already passed around too many times unless a manager overrides it.

Rebuilt on an authoritative roster rather than inference. Earlier versions inferred each CSM's service tier from account data. The rebuild pulls it from the HR system of record, surfaced through the data warehouse, as the single source of truth. That fixed three cascading errors at once — tier was being inferred incorrectly, inactive CSMs were inflating apparent capacity, and new hires with zero accounts were invisible to the tool entirely, which is exactly the population a capacity view exists to surface.

Going multi-user surfaced a class of problem single-user tools never hit. Rolled out to seven managers as a shared project at view-level permission — not a file passed around — which meant each user's environment had to be validated independently rather than assumed. Environment parity, warehouse read access, and connector configuration all had to be resolved at the group level rather than patched per person, and one shared dependency was replaced with a direct query path so the tool didn't rest on it.

Multi-user deployment turned out to be a permissions and environment problem rather than a distribution one. That lesson drove the next move: from a shared Claude project to a published artifact that runs on each viewer's own connectors (CS platform, CRM, Drive, Slack, email), so access and permissions are per user rather than borrowed from the builder.

Renewal Review Workbench

Live CRM + CS-platform renewal surface · shared review log · CSM and renewal-AE context on one card · Slack pre-reads and recaps

CS managers run a monthly renewal review with cross-functional partners. It ran on a static spreadsheet. Meetings spent their time clicking into CRM opportunities and CS-platform account pages to find the CSM's view, the renewal AE's notes, sentiment, and contract structure, and action items from one review had no reliable home for the next. Leadership decided to move the review onto the workbench and retire the spreadsheet.

The review runs end to end in one surface. A manager filters to a team and a review window, or clicks review priority: this quarter plus the next two, largest available-to-renew first, with minimum-value bands. They star accounts for the agenda and post a pre-read request to the team's Slack, grouped by CSM, asking each CSM to update their renewal notes in the CS platform by T-2. On refresh, those notes land on each review card next to the renewal AE's notes from the CRM, available-to-renew, forecast, win probability, sentiment, renewal propensity, delinquency, expansion opportunities, and business-review coverage. Decisions and next actions are logged on the card into a shared store every manager sees live. Afterward, a recap posts to Slack, an exec summary goes out by email, or the read-out exports.

Most of the work after launch was making it hold up for people other than me.

One unreadable field used to blank the page. CRM profiles differ in which fields each user can read, and a single unreadable optional field failed the whole query for that person. Fields are now split into required and optional. When the CRM rejects an optional field, the page drops exactly that field, remembers it for that browser, keeps working, and shows a banner naming what is missing and what is still safe to use. Money fields that disappear get a banner, not a footnote, so nobody reads a missing forecast as a zero.

Families roll up. A customer split across branch or billing-only child accounts showed the parent at a fraction of its real renewal. Accounts now roll up to one family line with combined value, and the children stay expandable.

A manager found the window bug. One CSM's book showed 5 of 18 accounts because the load window was too narrow, so multi-year renewals were never loaded. The window now covers every open renewal from the start of the fiscal year forward, taking scope from ~2,800 to ~6,200 opportunities, and the review window became a filter rather than a load limit.

Live sync that silently wasn't. A shared-store subscription never attached because several consumers raced the same async initializer. Notes were saving, but nobody received anyone else's updates live. Caching the initialization promise fixed it.

A build stamp on every page, so a screenshot always says which version produced it. It became the first troubleshooting step when two people see different numbers.

One region ran its internal renewal-sync tracker as a separate tool. I folded it in rather than shipping another link, scoped to exactly what was asked: that region, above a value threshold, renewals within three quarters, opt-in per viewer.

View implementation
optional-fields.js
// One unreadable optional field used to kill the whole page.
const REQUIRED = [/* id, close date, stage, amount, owner ... */];
let   OPTIONAL = [/* forecast, win probability, ... */];

async function load() {
  for (;;) {
    try { return await query(REQUIRED.concat(OPTIONAL)); }
    catch (e) {
      const f = optionalFieldNamedIn(e.message); // exact match only
      if (!f) throw e;                // real outage: surface it
      OPTIONAL = OPTIONAL.filter(x => x !== f);
      rememberDropped(f);             // next load skips it upfront
      banner(`Can't read ${f} on your profile. Everything else is safe to use.`);
    }
  }
}

Enterprise KPI Command Center

One shared, dated snapshot of the segment's KPIs · the accounts behind every number · each manager's open work in one place

KPI reporting lived in aging dashboards inside the CS platform, and the same KPI produced different numbers depending on report, filter, and rollup. Managers had no single place showing their KPIs, the specific accounts behind each one, and the open work that would move them. One example motivated it: business-review completion measured from task closure read far higher than business-review coverage measured from reviews actually held in the trailing twelve months. Same activity, two very different answers.

A contributor refreshes from the CS platform, and the page saves one dated snapshot to a shared store, so every manager sees the same numbers with the same as-of stamp. A duplicate-load guard exists because a snapshot day loaded twice doubles every count. When the platform rejects aggregate queries, the page pulls raw rows and totals them itself so the numbers still match. Fiscal quarters resolve from the CRM's period object rather than calendar math.

It carries six KPIs, each with a built-in target, color-coded green, within 80%, or below: accounts engaged this quarter; risk-plan coverage on low-propensity accounts, meaning an open risk plan and a customer touch in the last 30 days; sentiment currency, reviewed within 90 days; sentiment mismatch, low-propensity accounts still marked green, which is where surprise churn comes from; utilization recovery on accounts in the red usage band; and business-review coverage over 12 months.

The data health tab lists every account excluded from the KPIs, why, and the fix. The most common reason a manager's book looked empty was a missing account-relationship setting, not missing work. Every number links to the account in the source system, and every definition lives on the page, including how and why it can differ from other reports.

// Every manager reads ONE dated snapshot, not their own live pull.
async function refresh() {
  if (await lockHeldByOther()) return readSnapshot();
  const rows = await pullAllRows();          // 1,000-row windows, paged
  if (alreadyLoaded(rows.snapshotDate))      // a day loaded twice
    throw new Error("duplicate load");       // doubles every count
  await writeSnapshot({ rows, asOf: now(), by: me() });
}
Outcome

In pilot with leadership. Data accuracy validated with my manager ahead of review by the KPI program leads: two calculation bugs found and fixed during validation, and sources found for KPIs that had previously been link-outs only. The measurement plan is managers using it weekly, the segment's monthly scorecard sourced from its snapshot, legacy dashboards retired, and movement on the coverage KPIs once managers can see the accounts behind each gap.

Est. Not quantified yet. The claim it is built to test is that KPIs move when managers can see the specific accounts behind each gap, not just the percentage.

A KPI tool is a definitions project first. One number everywhere is the requirement; the dashboard is the delivery mechanism.

CS Carve Studio

Annual book-of-business carve · real books against agreed rules · scenarios, auto-balance and side-by-side compare · run jointly with RevOps

The annual carve decides which CSM owns which customer for the year. Leaders' feedback on the prior cycle was that the data came too late and hurt the start of the year. Books were balanced by hand, with no shared way to apply caps, renewal and expansion locks, alignment to sales pods, or account complexity, and no way to compare scenarios.

Account data comes from the warehouse table the sales carve already uses, with ownership taken from the book owner rather than the CRM account owner, which proved unreliable for this book. Each territory carries its rules from the requirements doc: family cap, target ARR, disruption cap, renewal-lock window, region share, maximum sales managers per book, and a book model.

Named scenarios add open reqs, departures, and capacity changes. Auto-balance moves whole unlocked families out of books that break a rule into the best-fit book in the same territory, preferring the same manager, the family's sales pod, region, and vertical, inside the disruption cap. It saves nothing until the preview is confirmed. Up to four scenarios compare side by side, everything is logged with undo, and CSV goes in and out for anyone working in a sheet.

Locks cover renewals inside a window before go-live, large open expansion pipeline, and manager locks that require a logged reason to override. The pipeline lock reuses the sales carve's own soft-lock, so CS and Sales agree on which accounts stay put.

Books have identities separate from people. Each book carries a stable ID independent of the CSM assigned to it. In territory-book segments a departing CSM's book stays intact and passes whole to the backfill; in named-book segments the book follows the CSM. Open reqs become empty books at ramp capacity, and auto-balance keeps high-complexity families away from ramping CSMs. That gives leaders the standard answer they asked for to "what happens when someone leaves mid-year." It uses the same complexity score as the assignment workbench.

Outcome

Surfaced two decisions before sign-off instead of after. At current caps, most books in two segments exceed the ARR limit, which makes it a headcount input to Finance rather than a balancing problem. And the rule limiting sales managers per CSM conflicts with the disruption cap, because every team in one segment covers the same sales managers. Leaders now review real books months ahead of go-live. The carve itself is in progress.

Est. Not quantified. The measurable claim is timing: decisions made weeks earlier than the prior cycle, with no unplanned re-carve in the first quarter. That will be reported when it happens.

Stakeholder Briefing Agents — and the Program That Generates Them

Multi-step read-only Claude agent → team deployment → a generator that builds a tailored briefing agent per stakeholder · live across three organizations

It started as one agent for me: a morning read across five systems I'd otherwise check by hand — Calendar, Granola, Gmail, Slack, and Glean, including a Security-approved path to government-cloud ticketing. It filters, de-dupes, prioritizes, and returns a ~2-minute briefing with a draft reply and a source link on every item.

Then my whole team ran on it. Then it stopped being a tool and became a program. The per-user config block that made it delegable was the actual product, so I built the layer above it: a project that generates a purpose-built briefing agent for any stakeholder — their systems, their lookback window, their priorities, their definition of what counts as urgent. The marginal cost of the next agent is a configuration pass, not a build.

It's live across three organizations — Customer Success, Implementation Services, and Sales — which is the part that matters. A briefing agent that works for a CS manager and a briefing agent that works for an implementation lead are different agents; what generalizes is the generator, not the output.

Two failure modes a naive version would have, both solved at the generator level so every agent inherits the fix. Name ambiguity: a first-name-only reference gets resolved against sent DMs and the directory, and in testing it correctly disambiguated four colleagues sharing one first name. Nagging about done work: it checks sent Slack and email before listing a task, which in one run cut the to-do list from four items to one.

Enterprise Renewal-Driver Analysis

Agentic Claude workflow wired to Salesforce · Databricks · Gainsight · Glean → size-controlled logistic regression → director-ready executive report

An end-to-end renewal-driver study run as a single agentic workflow. Claude pulled roughly 1,000 Enterprise renewal events across two years live from Salesforce, Databricks, and Gainsight, synthesized internal context through Glean, ran the statistics, and rendered a brand-matched executive report — a pipeline that would traditionally take an analyst, BizOps, and a designer.

The core is a size-controlled logistic regression: it isolated the two activities that stay predictive of renewal after controlling for account size, segment, and tenure — against a set of commonly assumed drivers that did not survive controls. I held the line on rigor throughout — reporting findings as predictive after controls, not causal, and reconciling to the certified rate rather than a convenient number.

View implementation
renewal-driver-analysis.py
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

# ~1,000 Enterprise renewals, 2 yrs — pulled live across
# Salesforce + Databricks + Gainsight by the agent
df = load_renewals(systems=["sfdc", "databricks", "gainsight"])

# which activities stay predictive of renewal AFTER
# controlling for account size, segment, tenure?
model = smf.logit(
    "renewed ~ exec_engagement + qbr_cadence + support_health"
    "  + onboarding_score + ...        # ~8 assumed drivers"
    "  + C(segment) + np.log(arr) + tenure_years",  # controls
    data=df,
).fit(disp=False)

# keep only drivers significant after controls — the signal
signal = model.pvalues[model.pvalues < 0.05].index.tolist()

Business-Review Scheduling — and the Incident That Tested It

Nightly production automation · incident diagnosis, windowed-firing fix, quantitative verification, and the comms that followed

The rolling Business Review (BR) automation for the Enterprise CS org — a nightly rule running against the full ~1,700-account book, scheduling every renewal and executive review the segment's 13 leaders manage against. The v2 design computes, per account, the next BR type, days until the next BR, fiscal quarter, workflow status, and eligibility flags, and checks for already-open follow-ups inside the data layer itself — which keeps the nightly firing rule a thin, auditable wrapper. It gets newly-eligible accounts the right task within 24 hours of crossing the threshold, with no duplicates. The build also corrected a tier filter that had been silently excluding a subset of higher-tier accounts in one region from executive reviews.

Then an upstream change broke it in production. A fiscal-year change to a service-tier threshold re-tiered roughly 500 accounts overnight. About 100 of them entered BR scope carrying stale anchor dates, so they computed as already overdue the moment they became eligible — a wave of spurious overdue tasks pointed straight at CSMs for work that was never actionable.

I confirmed the root cause quantitatively rather than asserting it: ~88% of the 114 problem accounts matched the re-tiered population. The fix was a windowed-firing gate on the live nightly rule, limiting fires to accounts within two weeks of their due date.

The gate — 100 accounts entering scope, sorted by the boundary
73 accounts suppressedBorn overdue. Stale anchor dates from a tier change, never actionable.
27 accounts firedLegitimately inside the two-week window.
← 2+ years overdue cut at −14 days due date →

Then I verified it against the actual fire-set exports, including boundary behavior: an account just inside the window fired, one just outside was suppressed, the boundary was crisp, and the worst case — an account computing more than two years overdue — was absent from the fire set entirely.

    br_eligible        = 1

AI governance

Governing AI across the org

Building AI tooling is one problem. Keeping an organization's AI tooling trustworthy once many people are building it is a different one, and it isn't solved by building more. As AI tooling scaled across the org, I proposed the governance model, and I now co-lead the program implementing it.

The pattern

Tooling spreads faster than ownership

AI artifacts get built in parallel across an org, by the people with the context and the initiative to build them — often inside workstreams that already have an owner. Past a certain level of adoption this happens by default. It isn't a discipline problem; it's a missing operating model.

The risk

Fragmentation, not capability

Ungoverned AI-artifact proliferation creates org-wide fragmentation risk: multiple tools computing the same metric, no agreed source of truth, no defined owner for validation, and no named owner to arbitrate when two tools disagree. None of that shows up in any single tool's quality.

The proposal

An intake and governance operating model

Who may build. Who validates. Where the source of truth lives. How a tool gets published, and who owns it after. I took it to leadership as a set of decisions to be made, with options and tradeoffs — not as a problem to be raised.

The proposal became a program. I co-lead a CS-wide AI intake and adoption pillar covering every CS segment and Services: a charter, a risk-tiered governance framework, an inventory of what's been built, one intake path, and a measurement model. Early discovery found at least three workflows where two or three people had independently built their own version of the same tool.

The measurement model

Measure adoption, not usage

Three layers, reported separately and never blended. Tool usage: is it being opened, and by whom. Diagnostic only, per tool, never summed across tools. Workflow adoption: has the intended workflow moved into the tool and stopped happening elsewhere, against a stated denominator. This is the measure of record. Business value: did the workflow get better, shown as a before/after per promoted tool and validated by users confirming the old process stopped.

The finding that shaped it: AI spend is driven by model choice and artifact building, not frequency of use, so ranking teams by spend ranks them by how much they build. Usage was already high. The gap was governance and measurement at the workflow layer, not adoption.

The test

The failure mode to test for

A tool that saves time on paper while users keep a parallel spreadsheet and reconcile between the two costs time instead of saving it. No time saving is claimed until users confirm the old process stopped.

Trust by design is what makes a single agent safe to run. An intake model, a validation owner, and an agreed source of truth are what make an organization's AI tooling safe to run at scale.

Principles

How I work

Five patterns run through most of the projects above.

Build, prove, then hand off

Most projects followed the same arc: build the tooling, validate it in production, then standardize it or hand it off as scope grew. The endpoint is other people's daily process, not a handoff document. The account-assignment tool went out to seven managers as a shared, permissioned project. The briefing agent went further: from my morning, to my team, to a generator that builds one per stakeholder across three organizations — at which point the thing I'd built was the generator, not the agent. The renewal review workbench and the assignment workbench now run on each person's own connectors across multiple segments, and the carve studio is used jointly with RevOps, so the endpoint for several builds is other teams' operating process. Several of these jobs now run as scheduled tasks I don't touch, which is the version of success where the tool stops being the point.

Engineer under the real constraints

I work within the actual limits. I rerouted ticketing and wiki queries off a connector a government-cloud environment couldn't support to the Glean MCP, split one program dashboard into two because a single one would have blown the platform's hard widget ceiling, edited registered templates in place rather than copying them because dynamic bindings had to survive the rebrand, fixed stale-closure bugs with useReducer, and isolated integration failures with a safeMcp() wrapper.

Trust by design — and at org scale

Read-only agents. Grounding rules that allow only facts the system actually fetched, with no invented names or dates. Name disambiguation, checks for work already done, and a human in the loop throughout. The same question scales up: once many people are building AI tooling, keeping it trustworthy stops being a build practice and becomes an intake model, a validation owner, and an agreed source of truth.

Quantify where it counts

A root cause confirmed at an ~88% match rather than asserted. A production fix verified against the actual fire-set exports, boundary behavior included, in both directions. A backfill audited to ~98% complete with the 14 missed accounts listed. A scope pressure-tested against a 1,922-row usage log, which showed the assigned row named four of seven live templates. I measure each build and report it as it is — including when the honest result is qualitative, like a director pulling a timeline forward. For the newer tools, the measurement plan was written before the results exist (baseline, a stated denominator, and targets at 90 days, 6 months and 12 months), so the outcome gets reported as it lands, including if it doesn't.

Ground it or don't claim it

I correct my own AI tooling when it reasons from plausibility instead of verified system state, and I've pushed back on it repeatedly in a single session until its conclusions were grounded in what the systems actually said. Facts get fetched before they're stated in anything outbound. A single-day measurement gets published as a 24-hour sample, not extrapolated into a daily average. Where a figure came from someone else's estimate, it gets labeled as theirs. The working rule is to treat my own AI output as unverified until it's checked against system state.
